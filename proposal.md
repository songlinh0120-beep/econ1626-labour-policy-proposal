# Recommendations and specific practices for the Australian labour market to address the AI revolution
###To: Australian Department of Employment and Workplace Relations  
###Policy focus: Labour market adjustment, skills policy, and responsible workplace AI adoption


## Executive Summary
AI will not only replace people's jobs but also reassign tasks to workers and businesses. Therefore, the market risk facing Australia is not mass unemployment, but uneven distribution of tasks, wage pressures on day-to-day jobs, and uneven access to AI-driven productivity gains. Based on economic evidence, labor demand declines when automation replaces labor tasks, but employment can recover when new tasks are created and workers are able to transfer to them. Therefore, the Australian government should adopt targeted AI task transformation strategies, such as identifying tasks exposed to AI, funding practical retraining, and supporting businesses to use AI to enhance productivity rather than simply replace labor. It not only makes good use of AI to improve productivity, but also protects employees who may be affected by AI and uneven distribution of tasks.


## Problem Definition and Background
Generative AI is transforming work because it is able to perform cognitive tasks that previously required human judgment, verbal expression, or technical knowledge. Unlike the earlier wave of computerization, which mainly affected everyday paperwork and manual work, AI can also engage in unconventional cognitive tasks such as writing, programming, analyzing, and making professional suggestions for certain problems. However, the work itself consists of a set of tasks in which relatively fixed steps can be performed by AI, but when it comes to communication between people or decisions on important matters, such as customer relations and strategic advice, these still require human judgment. This means that policies should not simply view careers as "safe" or "doomed". It is more reasonable to identify which tasks AI can replace and which tasks the role of workers may be enhanced.

The Australian context makes this urgent. Jobs and Skills Australia's generative AI capability study understands labour market impact as a combination of exposure, adoption and adaptation, rather than looking at technology exposure alone. According to research, generative AI is more likely to enhance many jobs than replace them completely, but the proportion of AI in this job is inconsistent across different occupations and industries. So when AI can help workers complete tasks better, it is complement, but when companies can use AI to complete the same output and reduce labor input, it is substitute.


## Impact Analysis
The first effect is task substitution. The frameworks of Acemoglu and Restrepo distinguish between displacement effect and reinstatement effect. The displacement effect refers to the substitution of labor for the tasks originally performed by capital, while the reinstatement effect refers to the creation of new tasks and the re-enhancement of labor demand. The biggest problem for enterprises is not automation itself, but "so-so automation", that is, AI technology used to replace labor but only bring moderate productivity gains. This is also true, and if businesses use chatbots, automated scheduling, AI monitoring, or foundational content generation to reduce manpower instead of redesigning efforts around higher-value human tasks, this will only lead to limited productivity gains.

The second effect is the extremization of skills. Generative AI has further amplified this view as a result of studies showing that computers often replace routine tasks based on clear rules while complementing unconventional problem-solving and communication tasks. This also makes it easy for some entry-level tasks such as junior analysts, paralegals, customer service staff, marketing assistants, and administrative staff to be replaced by AI. When a profession that requires long-term training has been replaced by AI at the entry stage, the number of talents with advanced skills required for the profession will gradually decrease, and the profession will be weakened.

The third effect is the unequal distribution of productivity gains. Empirical research suggests that AI can enhance productivity, but the benefits depend on the context. However, Dell'Acqua et al. have proposed the concept of "jagged technological frontiers", that is, when artificial intelligence completes certain tasks, it is not accurate in tasks that require contextual judgment. Therefore, policies should support not only digital skills in general, but also AI literacy. Let employees know when to rely on AI, when to validate AI, and when to use their own judgment.
